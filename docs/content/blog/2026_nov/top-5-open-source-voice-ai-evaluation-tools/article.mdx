A voice agent passes every text test you wrote for it. The prompts are tight,
the tools are wired up, and the transcripts read perfectly. Then it goes live
on a phone line, and callers hang up because it takes two seconds to answer,
talks over them when they pause to think, and mishears "fifteen" as "fifty".

None of those failures show up in a transcript. A transcript tells you what was
said, but not how it sounded, when each side spoke, or whether the agent heard
the caller correctly in the first place. That is why voice AI needs its own
evaluation tooling, and why a text-only eval suite gives voice teams false
confidence.

This guide compares the best open-source voice AI evaluation tools in 2026
based on what matters when you are actually shipping voice agents: audio-native
scoring, timing, transcription, realistic simulated callers, and a workflow
your engineers will run on every change.

## What is a voice AI evaluation tool?

A **voice AI evaluation tool** tests a voice agent against repeatable scenarios
and scores the resulting calls. Because a voice agent is a live call rather
than a function you call with a string, a complete evaluation covers three
things at once: **what was said** (the transcript), **how it sounded** (the
audio on both sides of the call), and **when it happened** (latency,
turn-taking, and interruptions).

In practice, that means placing simulated calls to your agent, speaking each
caller turn out loud, capturing the agent's spoken replies, and scoring the
conversation with both conversational metrics and audio metrics. The best
tools do this against the agent you actually deploy, over the same transport it
uses in production.

A voice AI evaluation tool is not the same thing as:

- **A speech model benchmark**, which compares STT or TTS providers on word
  error rate or naturalness, independent of your agent.
- **A voice agent framework**, such as LiveKit Agents or Pipecat, which you use
  to build the agent. Some frameworks ship testing modules, which is why two of
  them appear on this list.
- **A managed voice testing platform**, which runs closed-source simulations
  and dashboards as a hosted service. This comparison only covers tools you can
  install, inspect, and run yourself.
- **An LLM evaluation framework** that only scores text. If your agent is
  text-first, see our
  [Top 5 LLM evaluation frameworks](/blog/top-5-llm-evaluation-frameworks)
  comparison instead.

The primary job of a voice AI evaluation tool is to tell you whether a new
prompt, model, voice, or pipeline change made your voice agent better or worse
before real callers find out.

## What to look for in a voice AI evaluation tool

These are the criteria worth checking before committing to a tool:

1. **Audio-native scoring** – does it score the audio itself, such as voice
   naturalness, intelligibility, and glitches, or only the transcript?
2. **Timing** – can it measure response latency, turn-taking, and how the agent
   handles barge-in and overlapping speech?
3. **Transcription accuracy** – can it tell you whether the agent's own
   speech-to-text heard the caller correctly, so you can separate "misheard"
   from "misunderstood"?
4. **Simulated callers** – can it generate realistic callers with personas,
   voices, background noise, and interruption behavior, rather than replaying
   one clean script?
5. **Stack coverage** – does it work across voice platforms and transports
   such as Vapi, ElevenLabs, LiveKit, Pipecat, and raw WebSockets, or only with
   one framework?
6. **Conversation-level metrics** – can the same run also score task
   completion, role adherence, tool use, and relevancy across every turn?
7. **Developer workflow** – can evals run locally, in pytest or CI, and inside
   AI coding agents such as Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex?
8. **Path to production** – can the same metrics follow your agent into
   production monitoring once it takes real calls?

Most tools in this category are strong on two or three of these and silent on
the rest. The biggest dividing line is the first criterion: many "voice" eval
tools turn the call into text and then run text evals, which means they cannot
catch the failures that make callers hang up.

## 1. DeepEval

### What is DeepEval and why should voice teams consider it?

[DeepEval](https://github.com/confident-ai/deepeval) is an open-source,
Python-native evaluation framework for AI agents, voice agents, multi-turn
chatbots, RAG pipelines, and more. It works like pytest for AI applications,
and with more than 19,000 GitHub stars it is the most-starred tool in this
comparison.

Its main advantage for voice teams is that its evals are voice-native. DeepEval
attaches the audio from each turn of a call to a `ConversationalTestCase`, then
scores [what was said, how it sounded, and when it
happened](/docs/evaluation-voice) in the same run. Nine dedicated voice metrics
cover voice naturalness, speech intelligibility, voice consistency, audio
integrity, audio glitches, turn-taking naturalness, agent responsiveness,
transcription accuracy, and an overall voice reliability score. The transcript
is still judged by DeepEval's existing multi-turn metrics, such as
[turn relevancy](/docs/metrics-turn-relevancy), goal accuracy, and tool use, so
one test case answers both "did it solve the caller's problem" and "would a
caller have stayed on the line".

DeepEval also places the calls for you. Its
[conversation simulator in voice mode](/docs/conversation-simulator-voice-mode)
speaks each simulated caller turn through a TTS model, reaches your deployed
agent through a connector, and transcribes the agent's replies. Native
[voice agent connectors](/integrations/voice-agent-connectors/vapi) cover Vapi,
ElevenLabs, LiveKit, and Pipecat, plus any raw-audio WebSocket or in-process
Python callable. Each caller's voice,
background noise, and willingness to barge in come from a
[persona](/docs/conversation-simulator-voice-personas), so you can test the
impatient caller in a noisy car as easily as the patient one in a quiet room.

Voice support is currently in beta and Python-only. If you're already in
production, [Confident AI](https://www.confident-ai.com/products/voice-ai)
surfaces failing production calls before anyone has written an eval for them
and turns them into goldens for your next DeepEval run. DeepEval and Confident
AI are separate products: the former is the open-source framework for
evaluating in development, while the latter adds voice-native monitoring once
your agent takes real calls.

### Key features

- **Nine voice-native metrics** scored on call audio, from voice naturalness and
  audio glitches to [turn-taking naturalness](/docs/metrics-turn-taking-naturalness)
- **Transcription accuracy** that compares what the caller said with what the
  agent's own speech-to-text heard
- **Voice simulation** that places live calls to your deployed agent
- **Personas** with voice, background noise, speak-first, hang-up, and
  [interruption](/docs/conversation-simulator-voice-interruptions) behavior
- **Connectors** for Vapi, ElevenLabs, LiveKit, Pipecat, WebSockets, and
  in-process callables
- **Pluggable TTS and STT models**, with integrations for ElevenLabs, Cartesia,
  Deepgram, AssemblyAI, and OpenAI
- **The full multi-turn metric library** on the same `ConversationalTestCase`
- **Pytest integration** through `deepeval test run`, plus an Agent Skill for
  Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex
- **Call recordings and per-turn audio** saved locally for debugging failures

### Getting started

Install DeepEval and point the simulator at your voice agent. This example
calls an ElevenLabs conversational agent and scores turn-taking:

```python
from deepeval import evaluate
from deepeval.dataset import ConversationalGolden
from deepeval.metrics import TurnTakingNaturalnessMetric
from deepeval.simulator import ConversationSimulator
from deepeval.voice import ElevenLabsConnector, VoiceConfig

golden = ConversationalGolden(
    scenario="The user asks three short follow-up questions about a delivery.",
    expected_outcome="The agent answers each question in turn.",
)

simulator = ConversationSimulator(
    voice_config=VoiceConfig(
        connector=ElevenLabsConnector(agent_id="your-agent-id"),
    )
)
test_cases = simulator.simulate([golden])

evaluate(test_cases=test_cases, metrics=[TurnTakingNaturalnessMetric(threshold=0.6)])
```

Swap in a `LiveKitConnector` or a WebSocket connector to test agents built on
other stacks. Using an AI coding agent? Follow the
[5-minute Agent Skill quickstart](/docs/vibe-coder-quickstart) and ask it to add
voice evals to your repo.

## 2. LiveKit Agents

### What is LiveKit Agents and why should voice teams consider it?

[LiveKit Agents](https://github.com/livekit/agents) is an open-source framework
for building realtime voice AI agents, with about 15,000 GitHub stars and an
Apache 2.0 license. It ships its own [testing and
evaluation](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/start/testing/) module, which makes
it the most natural starting point if your agent already runs on LiveKit.

Unit tests run a single conversation turn in text mode with pytest or Vitest,
asserting on messages, tool calls, and handoffs. For full conversations,
`livekit.agents.evals` provides eight built-in LLM judges, including accuracy,
relevancy, safety, tool use, handoffs, and task completion, which run together
through a `JudgeGroup`. [Agent
simulations](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/start/testing/simulations/) add an
LLM-driven caller that follows a persona and goal from a `scenarios.yaml` file,
and the CLI can generate scenarios from your agent's source.

The trade-off is that the open-source pieces grade text. Audio simulations,
which score turn-taking, interruptions, transcription accuracy, and latency,
run on LiveKit Cloud and are metered at a higher rate than text runs, and
`JudgeGroup` and custom judges are Python-only. LiveKit itself recommends text
simulations on every commit and audio runs nightly or before release.

### Key features

- Text-mode unit tests in pytest and Vitest with message, tool call, and
  handoff assertions
- Eight built-in LLM judges and custom judges through a `Judge` subclass
- Multi-turn simulations with LLM-driven callers from YAML scenarios
- Scenario generation from agent source code
- Repeated sampling with a configurable pass rate for non-deterministic runs
- Audio-mode simulations for timing and transcription on LiveKit Cloud

### Pricing

| Product                 | Price                     | Includes                                                       |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| LiveKit Agents          | Free and open source      | Agent framework, unit tests, judges, and text-mode evaluation  |
| LiveKit Cloud simulations | Usage-based             | Hosted simulations, with audio runs metered at a higher rate   |

**LiveKit Agents is for you if...**

- Your voice agent is built on LiveKit and will stay there
- You want fast, cheap text-mode tests that run with your existing pytest or
  Vitest suite
- You are comfortable running audio evaluations on LiveKit Cloud

**LiveKit Agents is not for you if...**

- Your agent runs on Vapi, ElevenLabs, Pipecat, or a custom stack
- You need open-source audio-quality metrics such as naturalness or glitches
- You want one evaluation suite across voice and non-voice applications

## 3. Pipecat Evals

### What is Pipecat Evals and why should voice teams consider it?

[Pipecat](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat) is an open-source framework for
voice and multimodal agents, with about 17,000 GitHub stars and a BSD 2-Clause
license. [Pipecat Evals](https://docs.pipecat.ai/pipecat/evals/overview) is its
built-in system for testing agent behavior, and it runs scenarios against the
same pipeline and services you deploy.

Scenarios live in YAML. Scripted scenarios define the caller's turns and the
events you expect back, such as text that should appear, a function call with
specific arguments, or a reply within a latency budget set by `within_ms`.
Simulated scenarios give an LLM a persona and goal, then judge a success
criterion and optional scored metrics. Text mode skips STT and TTS for speed,
while audio mode synthesizes the caller's voice and transcribes the agent's
reply, and `send_after` lets the harness barge in mid-response to test
recovery.

Pipecat Evals is deliberately local-first: the judge and the simulated caller
default to a local model through Ollama, so a suite can run without any API
keys beyond your agent's own. Its scope is development, and Pipecat's own docs
point teams to third-party platforms for production testing and monitoring.

### Key features

- YAML scenarios that mix scripted turns and LLM-simulated callers
- Assertions on text, function calls and arguments, and latency budgets
- Barge-in testing through `send_after`
- Text and audio modes, with audio-only evaluation for speech-to-speech agents
- Local judge and persona models through Ollama by default
- Concurrent suites from a manifest, plus a Python library

### Pricing

| Product       | Price                | Includes                                                        |
| ------------- | -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pipecat Evals | Free and open source | YAML scenarios, simulations, judges, and text and audio modes   |
| Model usage   | Your provider costs  | STT, TTS, and LLM usage, or free local judges through Ollama    |

**Pipecat Evals is for you if...**

- Your voice agent is built on Pipecat
- You want scenario files that live next to your pipeline and run locally
- You want latency budgets and barge-in checks without extra infrastructure

**Pipecat Evals is not for you if...**

- Your agent is not built on Pipecat
- You need audio-quality or transcription-accuracy metrics
- You want a path from development tests to production monitoring

## 4. LangWatch

### What is LangWatch and why should voice teams consider it?

[LangWatch](https://github.com/langwatch/langwatch) is an open-source LLM
observability and evaluation platform under the Apache 2.0 license. Its
[agent simulation library](https://github.com/langwatch/scenario), with about
1,000 GitHub stars, added [voice agent
testing](https://scenario-docs.langwatch.ai/voice/getting-started) in 2026.

Its strength is breadth of platforms. LangWatch drives a synthesized caller
through a multi-turn conversation with agents on ElevenLabs, OpenAI Realtime,
Gemini Live, Vapi, LiveKit, Pipecat, and Twilio, and it can wrap a text-only
agent in an STT-to-LLM-to-TTS chain. Simulated callers take a voice, a persona,
and audio effects such as background noise and phone-line quality, and runs
save a full recording of the call.

Scoring comes from a judge agent that checks the conversation against criteria
you write, with a per-turn verdict. There is no dedicated catalog of audio,
timing, or transcription metrics, so measuring how the agent sounded or how
fast it answered is left to the criteria you define. Tests run in pytest or
Vitest, and results can flow into the wider LangWatch platform.

### Key features

- Voice adapters for ElevenLabs, OpenAI Realtime, Gemini Live, Vapi, LiveKit,
  Pipecat, and Twilio
- Simulated callers with voices, personas, background noise, and phone quality
- Judge agents with per-turn verdicts against custom criteria
- Interruption recipes for barge-in and cancellation
- Python and TypeScript SDKs with pytest and Vitest
- Call recordings and live transcripts for debugging

### Pricing

| Product            | Price                | Includes                                                   |
| ------------------ | -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| LangWatch Scenario | Free and open source | Voice simulations, judge agents, pytest and Vitest         |
| LangWatch platform | Self-hosted or cloud | Observability, evaluations, and team workflows             |

**LangWatch is for you if...**

- You test agents across several voice platforms and realtime APIs
- You want simulated callers with noisy, phone-quality audio
- You prefer writing your own pass criteria over a metric library

**LangWatch is not for you if...**

- You need standardized audio-quality, timing, or transcription metrics
- You want research-backed conversational metrics out of the box
- You want voice-native scoring rather than criteria-based judging

## 5. ServiceNow EVA

### What is ServiceNow EVA and why should voice teams consider it?

[EVA](https://github.com/ServiceNow/eva) is an MIT-licensed, end-to-end
framework from ServiceNow for evaluating conversational voice agents, released
alongside a [research paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13841) in 2026. It runs
bot-to-bot: a user simulator and the agent under test speak to each other over
a live WebSocket connection, with no humans in the loop.

EVA scores two dimensions. Accuracy covers task completion, faithfulness, and
agent speech fidelity, an audio LLM judge currently in beta. Experience covers
conciseness, turn-taking, conversation progression, and latency. It ships 213
enterprise scenarios across airline customer service, healthcare HR, and IT
service management, and supports both cascade (STT, LLM, TTS) and
speech-to-speech architectures, with published results for 12 systems.

That makes EVA a benchmark first. It is excellent for comparing voice
architectures and models on a fixed, enterprise-flavored test set, but its
harness is Pipecat-based and it is not designed as a test suite you add to your
own agent's repository and run on every pull request.

### Key features

- Bot-to-bot evaluation over live WebSocket audio
- Accuracy and experience scores, including an audio speech-fidelity judge
- 213 enterprise scenarios across three domains
- Cascade and speech-to-speech architecture support
- Published leaderboard results for 12 voice systems
- CLI and Docker Compose for reproducible runs

### Pricing

| Product        | Price                | Includes                                          |
| -------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| ServiceNow EVA | Free and open source | Benchmark scenarios, metrics, and evaluation CLI  |
| Model usage    | Your provider costs  | STT, TTS, LLM, and speech-to-speech API usage     |

**ServiceNow EVA is for you if...**

- You are choosing between voice architectures or models
- You want a published, reproducible benchmark to compare against
- Your use case resembles customer service, HR, or IT service desks

**ServiceNow EVA is not for you if...**

- You need to test your own agent's scenarios on every change
- Your agent is not built on, or cannot be reached through, Pipecat
- You need a developer test workflow or a path to production

## Which voice AI evaluation tool should you choose?

### Step 1: Decide whether you are scoring transcripts or calls

Transcript-based evaluation turns a call into text and judges the text. It is
fast and cheap, and it catches wrong answers, missed tool calls, and policy
violations. Call-based evaluation also scores the audio and the timeline, which
is where latency, talking over callers, garbled speech, and mishearing show up.

If your callers' main complaints are about what the agent said, LiveKit Agents
or Pipecat Evals in text mode may be enough. If they hang up because of how the
agent sounded or when it spoke, you need voice-native scoring, which is where
DeepEval is strongest.

### Step 2: Check how locked-in your stack is

- **One framework, forever:** LiveKit Agents and Pipecat Evals are the
  lowest-friction options because they live inside the framework you already
  use.
- **Several platforms, or a likely migration:** DeepEval and LangWatch reach
  agents across Vapi, ElevenLabs, LiveKit, Pipecat, and custom transports, so
  your eval suite survives a change of voice platform.

### Step 3: Separate testing from benchmarking

A test suite runs your own scenarios against your own agent on every change.
A benchmark runs fixed scenarios to compare systems. ServiceNow EVA is a
benchmark; DeepEval, LiveKit Agents, Pipecat Evals, and LangWatch are test
suites. Many teams use a benchmark once to pick an architecture, then a test
suite every day after.

### Step 4: Plan the path to production

Pre-release tests only cover the calls you thought to simulate. Once real
callers arrive, you need production calls scored with the same metrics, and the
failing ones turned into new test cases. If you're already in production,
[Confident AI](https://www.confident-ai.com/products/voice-ai) provides
voice-native observability on top of DeepEval's metrics: it surfaces failing
production calls before anyone has written an eval for them and turns them into
goldens for your next run. LangWatch and LiveKit Cloud offer managed paths for
their own ecosystems.
